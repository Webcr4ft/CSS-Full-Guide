# Working with CSS Fonts

## What Are the Fundamentals of Typography?

Typography is the art of choosing the right fonts and formatting text so it is visually appealing and easy to read.

Typography can help you:
- Evoke emotions
- Establish visual hierarchy
- Reinforce a brand's identity
- Improve readability
- Make content easier to understand

---

# Typeface vs Font

## Typeface

A **typeface** is the overall design and style of a set of characters, numbers, and symbols.

Think of a typeface as the **blueprint or design family** for fonts.

Examples of typeface classifications include:
- Serif
- Sans Serif
- Script
- Blackletter
- Monospaced
- Decorative

## Font

A **font** is a specific variation of a typeface with specific characteristics, such as:
- Size
- Weight
- Style
- Width

For example, Times New Roman can have variations such as:
- Times New Roman Regular
- Times New Roman Bold
- Times New Roman Italic
- Times New Roman Bold Italic

---

# Serif Typefaces

A **Serif** typeface has small decorative lines at the ends of characters.

Example:

Hello, World!

Serif typefaces have a classical or traditional appearance.

They are commonly used for printed materials, such as books.

Examples:
- Times New Roman
- Georgia
- Garamond

---

# Sans Serif Typefaces

**Sans Serif** means "without serif."

Sans Serif typefaces do not have the small decorative lines at the ends of characters.

Example:

Hello, World!

Sans Serif typefaces have a more modern appearance and are commonly used in digital design because they are generally easy to read on screens.

Examples:
- Helvetica
- Arial
- Roboto

---

# Other Typeface Classifications

Other common typeface classifications include:
- Script

- Decorative

# Font Family Variations

Fonts within the same family can have different characteristics.

For example:
- Times New Roman
- Times New Roman Bold
- Times New Roman Italic
- Times New Roman Bold Italic

These variations typically involve:

## Weight

**Weight** refers to the thickness of the characters.

Examples:
- Light
- Regular
- Bold
- Black

## Style

**Style** refers to the slant and orientation of characters.

Examples:
- Italic
- Oblique

## Width

**Width** refers to the horizontal space occupied by characters.

Examples:
- Condensed
- Regular
- Expanded

# Fundamental Elements of Typography

There are several important terms used to describe the structure and positioning of characters.

## Baseline

The **baseline** is the imaginary horizontal line on which most characters rest.

## Cap Height

The **cap height** is the height of uppercase letters, measured from the baseline to the top of the uppercase letters.

## X-Height

The **x-height** is the average height of lowercase letters, excluding ascenders and descenders.

It is roughly represented by the height of the lowercase letter `x`.

## Ascenders

**Ascenders** are the parts of lowercase letters that extend above the x-height.

Examples:
- h
- b
- d
- f

## Descenders

**Descenders** are the parts of lowercase letters that extend below the baseline.

Examples:
- y
- g
- p
- q

# Advanced Typography Concepts

Three important typography spacing concepts are:
- Kerning
- Tracking
- Leading

---

# Kerning

**Kerning** is the adjustment of space between specific pairs of characters to improve readability and aesthetics.

For example, the space between the letters `A` and `V` may need adjustment because certain letter combinations can create awkward gaps.

Kerning adjusts individual character pairs rather than the entire block of text.

### Remember:
**Kerning = Space between specific pairs of letters**

---

# Tracking

**Tracking** is the adjustment of space between all characters in a block of text.

It affects how dense or open the text appears.

For example:

Normal:
Available

More tracking:
A v a i l a b l e

### Remember:
**Tracking = Overall character spacing**

Unlike kerning, tracking affects the spacing throughout the text instead of just one specific pair.

---

# Leading

**Leading** is the vertical space between lines of text.

It is measured from the baseline of one line to the baseline of the next line.

### Remember:
**Leading = Space between lines of text**

---

# Kerning vs Tracking vs Leading

| Concept | What it controls |
|---|---|
| Kerning | Space between specific character pairs |
| Tracking | Space between all characters |
| Leading | Vertical space between lines |

Easy way to remember:

**Kerning → Specific letters**

**Tracking → All letters**

**Leading → Lines**

# Line Length and Readability

**Line length** can have a significant impact on readability.

If text is **too narrow**:
- Readers need to refocus too often.
- Reading can become uncomfortable.

If text is **too wide**:
- Readers may experience fatigue.
- It can become harder to follow from one line to the next.

You should find an **optimal line length** that provides comfortable reading.

---

# Visual Hierarchy

Typography can be used to create a **visual hierarchy**.

Visual hierarchy helps readers understand which parts of the text or user interface are most important.

For example:

MAIN HEADING
↓
Important subheading
↓
Normal paragraph text

Different typography choices can make some information stand out more than others.

---

# Ways to Create Visual Hierarchy

## 1. Font Size

Larger fonts usually indicate that text is more important.

Example:

MAIN TITLE

Subtitle

Normal paragraph

The larger title attracts attention first.

## 2. Font Weight

Making text **bold** can help emphasize important information.

Example:

This is normal text.

This is bold and more noticeable.

## 3. Underlining

Underlining text can help highlight key points.

However, on websites, underlined text is often associated with links, so it should be used appropriately.

## 4. Italics

Italic text can help highlight or emphasize certain information.

Example:

This is emphasized text.

## 5. Spacing

Accurate spacing around text can highlight its importance.

Spacing can include:
- Margins
- Padding
- Line spacing
- Character spacing

Good spacing can make a design cleaner and easier to understand.

## 6. Color Contrast

Color contrast can bring readers' attention to specific parts of the text.

A contrasting color can help important information stand out from surrounding content.

Text should still have enough contrast with its background to remain readable.
